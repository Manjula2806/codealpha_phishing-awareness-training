Phishing is a message designed to make you reveal information, send money, or open a door to your device. The bait changes. The pressure usually doesn’t.

The useful ruleUnexpected + urgent + asks for action? Stop. Verify using a contact method you already trust.
01 / Read the message

Read past the display name.

Attackers borrow familiar brands and plausible situations. Look at the full sender address, the request, and where each link actually leads.

Action required: your mailbox will be closed today

ITIT Help Desk <helpdesk@company-support-login.com>
to: you · 9:14 AM

We detected unusual activity. Your access will be permanently suspended in 30 minutes. Confirm your password now to keep your account active.

Lookalike senderThe name says “IT Help Desk,” but the domain is not your organization’s.
Artificial urgencyA short deadline tries to replace careful thinking with panic.
Credential requestLegitimate support should not ask you to confirm a password through an email link.

02 / Check the website

A lock is not a verdict.

HTTPS encrypts a connection; it does not prove who owns the site. Read the domain from right to left and check the exact spelling before entering anything.

●  Expected domainhttps://accounts.example.com/sign-in

The registered domain is example.com.

●  Lookalike domainhttps://example.com.login-check.net/sign-in

The registered domain is login-check.net, not example.com.

A

Inspect the real address

Hover over a link on desktop or press and hold on mobile to preview it. Better: navigate using a bookmark or typed address.

B

Watch for subtle swaps

Extra words, misspellings, hyphens, odd subdomains, and lookalike characters can disguise a domain.

C

Question the context

A sign-in page reached from an unexpected attachment or message deserves a second look.

D

Use a second signal

Check with the organization through its known app, saved number, or official site, not the message’s contact details.

03 / Know the playbook

They target attention, not just software.

Social engineering uses human instincts to get around good technical defenses. Notice what the message is trying to make you feel.

!

Urgency & fear

“Your account closes in 10 minutes.” Pressure makes verification feel too slow.

★

Authority

“The CEO needs gift cards now.” A familiar title is used to discourage questions.

$

Reward & curiosity

Unexpected refunds, prizes, invoices, or “private” documents invite a quick click.

↗

Familiarity & routine

Delivery updates, shared files, payroll notices, and renewal reminders blend into the day.

REPORTED CASE2020
Real-world example · Twitter

A phone call can be the phishing message.

Attackers used social engineering against employees to gain access to internal tools, then took over high-profile accounts. The incident showed that a convincing human request can bypass safeguards even when no fake website is involved.

The incident is summarized from public reporting; examples above are common patterns, not quotes from the case.

04 / Make it harder

Build a small pause into the routine.

No single clue catches every attempt. A few reliable habits make stolen passwords and rushed decisions much less useful to an attacker.

REPORTED CASE2013–15
Real-world example · Business email compromise

Familiar invoices, changed bank details.

Fraudsters impersonated suppliers and sent forged invoices to Google and Facebook. The companies paid more than $100 million before the scheme was uncovered; U.S. authorities later announced the case. Verify payment changes using a known phone number, never just a reply to the email.

01 / VERIFY

Change channels

Call a known number or open the official app. Never use contact details supplied in the suspicious message.

02 / PROTECT

Use a password manager

It can help spot lookalike domains by refusing to autofill where your real login belongs.

03 / ADD A FACTOR

Turn on MFA or passkeys

Prefer phishing-resistant passkeys or security keys where available. Never share a one-time sign-in code.

04 / REPORT

Flag it, then delete

Use your organization’s report-phishing button or official reporting channel. Don’t forward suspicious links casually.

05 / IF YOU CLICKED

Act quickly, without shame

Tell your IT/security team or provider. Change reused passwords from a trusted device and revoke active sessions.

06 / BEFORE PAYING

Confirm high-risk requests

Verify new bank details, gift card requests, and urgent transfers through a separate established channel.

Case summary: U.S. Department of Justice public announcement concerning forged invoices and business email compromise.
